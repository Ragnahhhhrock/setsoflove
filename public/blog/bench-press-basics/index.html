Bench press basics: setup, safety and common mistakes

30 Oct 2026 · 4 min read · By the SetsOfLove team

Good bench press form starts before you touch the bar. Set your eyes under the bar, plant your feet, hold a steady upper back and keep the bar moving in a controlled path over your chest. Use safety bars or a spotter, start light, and get a qualified coach to check your technique.

How do you set up for the bench press?

Most problems in the lift come from a rushed setup. Take a minute and do it the same way each time.

  1. Lie down with your eyes roughly under the bar. If the bar is too far back or forward, unracking it gets awkward.
  2. Place your feet flat on the floor. Keep them where you can push into the ground without your hips lifting off the bench.
  3. Pull your shoulder blades back and down. Think of squeezing them towards the bench. This gives you a stable base and takes some strain off the fronts of your shoulders.
  4. Keep your head, upper back and hips on the bench. A small natural curve in your lower back is fine. Forcing a big arch isn't needed when you're learning.
  5. Take your grip, then unrack. Breathe in, lift the bar off the hooks and bring it over your chest with your arms straight.

Setup is a skill like any other. It feels fiddly for the first few weeks and then becomes automatic.

Where should your hands go?

Most people grip a little wider than shoulder width. A common check is that your forearms are close to vertical when the bar touches your chest, with your wrists stacked over your elbows.

  • Wrap your thumbs around the bar. A thumbless grip can let the bar slip, and that's a real risk over your face and chest.
  • Keep your wrists straight. If the bar sits back in your palm over your forearm, you'll feel it less in your wrists.
  • Use the same grip on both sides. Many bars have rings or marks to help. Check both hands before you lift.

If a grip feels sharp or uncomfortable in your wrists or shoulders, change it or ask for help. Don't push through it.

What does good bench press form look like?

The bar doesn't travel in a perfectly straight line, and it shouldn't. In most cases, it lowers to the lower part of your chest and presses back up and slightly towards your face, ending over your shoulders.

  • Lower the bar under control. Don't let it fall.
  • Touch your chest lightly, or stop just above it. Don't bounce it off your ribs.
  • Press back up with your feet still planted.
  • Finish with your arms straight and the bar stacked over your shoulders.

Your elbows should sit at a moderate angle to your body, roughly halfway between tucked in and flared out wide. Flaring them straight out to the sides is a common source of shoulder discomfort.

How do you stay safe on the bench press?

The bench press is the lift where the bar can end up over your chest and throat, so safety setup matters more than on most others.

  • Use safety bars (also called pins or arms) in a power rack, if one is available. Set them just below the lowest point of the bar. If you get stuck, you can lower the bar onto them.
  • If you use a spotter, agree the plan first. Tell them when you want a hand and what you'll say. A good spotter doesn't touch the bar until you ask or the bar stops.
  • Follow your gym's rules on collars and racks. If you're unsure, ask staff.
  • Don't lift alone near your limit. A lighter day is a fine day.
  • Ask before you borrow a spotter. Most gym-goers are happy to help, but it's polite to check, and polite to accept a no.

If you're lifting alone and the bar gets stuck, don't panic. Ask a coach or staff member to show you the right way to bail out in person, before you ever need it.

What are the most common bench press mistakes?

  • Bouncing the bar off the chest. It looks like extra power, but it removes control and adds risk.
  • Lifting your hips off the bench. This usually means the weight is too heavy for the setup.
  • Letting your wrists bend back. The bar should be stacked over your forearm.
  • Rushing the unrack. Take a breath and settle before the first rep.
  • Letting your shoulders roll forward. This undoes your upper back position.
  • Adding weight faster than your technique improves. This is the most common one.
  • Skipping the warm-up. A few lighter reps first helps you check your setup.

Most mistakes are about speed and ego. Slowing down tends to fix them.

How should you progress?

Add weight only when the last session felt controlled, and add it in small steps. Small steps can be as little as the smallest plates your gym has. If your form changes, go back to the previous weight. Tracking each session helps you see what's working. How to track your lifts explains what to record.

There's no rush, and there's no target number you need to hit. Rest matters too, and a sore day is a normal part of the process.

Why see a coach?

A qualified coach can see things you can't feel: uneven bar height, shifting hips, a wrist that bends. They can also tell you which version of the lift suits your body, and your shoulders in particular. That's worth more than any article, including this one.

If you feel sharp pain, pain that doesn't ease, or numbness or tingling in your arms or hands, stop and see a doctor or physio. Pain in the chest or difficulty breathing is urgent, so get help straight away.
