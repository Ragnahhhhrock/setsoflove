---
Dating while training for a marathon, a lifting meet or any other event is workable if you're upfront early. Tell the person what the block looks like and when it ends, then plan dates around your key sessions. Most people respect a clear end date far more than vague busyness.

## Why dating while training for a marathon needs a plan

An event block has a shape. It runs for a set number of weeks, it ends on a known date, and some weeks are much heavier than others. That is useful, because you can describe it in a sentence and the other person can see the edge of it.

Without a plan, 2 things tend to go wrong. You cancel on someone because a long session ran over. Or you skip a session because you feel guilty about cancelling. Both leave you tired and the other person unsure where they stand.

A plan fixes both. You decide in advance which sessions are fixed, which dates are easy to fit, and when you'll need to say no.

## Tell them early, with dates

You don't need to explain your whole program on a first date. You do need to say that an event is coming, once it's clear you'd like to see each other again.

Try something like this.

- "I'm training for a marathon on 14 Mar. Until then my weekends start early, so Friday nights are short for me."
- "I've got a lifting meet in 9 weeks. I'm strict about sleep until then, but I'm free most evenings."
- "The next 6 weeks are my busiest. After that I've got much more room."

Notice what these do. They give a date, they say what it means in practice, and they say what you still have to offer. They don't apologise.

Then ask about their own calendar. Someone with school-age kids, a rotating roster or a study load has constraints too. You're comparing 2 timetables, not defending yours.

## Map the block onto a calendar

Put the event date in your calendar and work backwards. Your coach or training plan will have the real phases. The table below shows how those phases usually affect dating, in general terms.

| Phase | What it usually feels like | What suits a date |
|---|---|---|
| Base or early build | Steady, manageable load | Most dates are fine, including evenings |
| Heavy weeks | Long sessions, more fatigue | Short dates, daytime, early finish |
| Peak | The biggest sessions of the block | Keep 1 or 2 low-effort plans in reserve |
| Taper | Lower load, more spare time, more nerves | Gentle walks, good food, good company |
| Event week | Routines matter, sleep matters | Keep it simple, or skip the date |
| Recovery | Tired, a bit flat, happy to rest | Slow dates, long breakfasts |

Your own plan may differ. If you're unsure how to set the load or how long to recover, ask a qualified coach. If something hurts in a way that worries you, see a doctor or physio.

## Dates that fit the plan

Pick dates that work with your week, not against it.

- **After a long session.** A late breakfast or a coffee is easy to do when you're already up and moving.
- **On rest days.** A market, a gallery or a slow walk uses the day without costing training. The [rest day date ideas](/blog/rest-day-date-ideas/) post has more.
- **Early evenings.** Book 6:00 pm instead of 8:30 pm and you still get your sleep.
- **A date that isn't the night before a key session.** Treat that night as protected.

Dinner is fine too. You don't have to avoid food or drinks to look disciplined. Order what you enjoy, and see what your coach suggests for fuelling on big training days.

## What the taper weeks are really like

The taper often surprises people. Training load drops, so you have spare time and spare energy. You may also feel restless and nervous, and a little irritable.

Say so. "I get twitchy in the last 2 weeks. It's not about you" is a kind thing to tell someone you're seeing. It lets them read your mood correctly.

This is also a good time to see someone. You have the hours, and a relaxed walk is a pleasant way to burn off nerves. Don't try anything new, though. A new cuisine or a late night out the week before the event is not a good plan.

## Event day and the week after

If the person wants to come and cheer, say yes or no plainly. Both answers are fine. Some people like having a friendly face at the finish. Others want to get through the day alone, and that's allowed.

If they do come, tell them what to expect. Where will you be? When will you finish? Will you want to talk afterwards, or will you need a shower and a sleep? A short note beforehand saves a lot of guessing.

Afterwards, expect a flat week. Many people feel tired, hungry and a little empty once the goal is gone. Keep your first plan after the event easy.

Then reset together. This is the point to talk about what the next season looks like, and how a normal week fits both of you. If you've started seeing each other seriously, [keeping your routine when you start seeing someone](/blog/keeping-your-routine-when-you-start-seeing-someone/) is a good next read.

## A quick worked example

Say your event is on a Sunday 12 weeks away, and you've been out twice with someone you like.

1. Tell them about the event and the date.
2. Mark your long sessions in the calendar and keep those mornings clear.
3. Offer 2 regular windows, such as Wednesday evenings and Saturday afternoons.
4. Agree that a last-minute cancel is fine when either of you is wiped out, with a quick message instead of silence.
5. Pencil in something relaxed for the Sunday after the event.

That is the whole system. If you need help phrasing the schedule talk, there's a longer guide to [explaining your training routine to someone new](/blog/explaining-your-routine-to-a-date/). And if your week is built around rosters, read about [dating when you work shifts and still train](/blog/dating-with-shift-work-and-training/).

The aim is simple. You show up for the event, and you show up for the person. A clear plan lets you do both.
