---
title: How to track your lifts
description: How to track workouts without fuss: notebook, notes app or spreadsheet, what to record each session, how to review monthly and why honest numbers matter.
---
The simplest way to track workouts is to write down the exercise, the weight, the reps and how the session felt, straight after you finish. A notebook, a notes app or a spreadsheet will all do the job. What matters is that you pick one, use it every session and record what actually happened.

## Why track workouts at all?

Memory is a poor record keeper. A week later, most of us can't say what we lifted, and a month later we're guessing. A log turns "I think it's going well" into something you can check.

Tracking helps in a few practical ways.

- **You know what to do next session.** No standing around trying to remember last week's weight.
- **You can see progress that's too slow to feel.** Change often shows up over months, not days.
- **You spot patterns.** Maybe a lift always feels rough after a bad night's sleep, or a certain exercise irritates a shoulder.
- **You have something to show a coach.** A short history makes a session with a coach far more useful.

It's also a kind way to treat yourself. A log reminds you what you've done, which is easy to forget on a flat day.

## Which tool should you use?

There's no best tool. The best one is the one you'll open.

- **Paper notebook.** Cheap, quick and works with sweaty hands. It doesn't run out of battery. The downside is that it's harder to search and easy to leave at home.
- **Notes app.** Always on your phone. You can copy last session's entry and edit it, which saves time. It gets messy over months unless you keep a consistent layout.
- **Spreadsheet.** Best if you like numbers. You can sort by exercise, add simple charts and keep years of history. It takes more setup and is fiddlier on a phone.

Start with the simplest option. You can always move your history to a spreadsheet later. If you try one for a month and keep skipping it, switch tools rather than blaming yourself.

## What should you record?

Keep it short enough that you'll actually do it. A useful entry has:

1. **The date.**
2. **The exercise name.** Use the same name each time, so you can find it later.
3. **The weight.** Write the unit, and note whether it includes the bar. If you use both kg and lb, see our guide to [converting kg to lb](/blog/kg-to-lb-converting-your-lifts/).
4. **The reps you completed.** The real number, not the planned one.
5. **A quick note on how it felt.** A word or two is enough: easy, solid, grindy, sore shoulder.

Optional extras, if you like them:

- Which bench, rack or machine you used, since settings vary between gyms.
- The setup details that matter for you, such as grip width or rack height.
- Sleep, stress or illness on the day.
- Any pain or niggle, and where.

Don't add things you won't use. A log that takes 10 minutes to fill in won't last.

## When should you write it down?

Right after each exercise, or straight after the session. Waiting until you get home means you'll forget details, or skip it. Some people jot the numbers between sets, which works if it doesn't slow the gym down for others. Don't hold a piece of equipment while you scroll or type. Step aside to record.

A handy habit is to write the plan before you start, using last session's entry. Then you only need to tick or correct it. That makes the log a plan and a record at once.

## How do you review your log?

Once a month, spend 10 minutes looking back. You're not marking yourself. You're checking for trends.

- **What went up, and what stayed the same?** A plateau is information, not failure.
- **Which lifts felt good, and which didn't?** Notes help here.
- **Did you train as often as you meant to?** Consistency tells you more than any single number. [Consistency over motivation](/blog/consistency-over-motivation/) covers building a routine that lasts.
- **Did any pain notes repeat?** If the same niggle keeps showing up, talk to a coach or physio.
- **What's one small thing to change next month?** Just one.

If a month looks flat, check sleep, stress and rest before you change your whole approach. Our piece on [rest, sleep and recovery for people who lift](/blog/rest-sleep-and-recovery-for-lifters/) is a good starting point.

## Why do honest numbers matter?

A log is only useful if it's true. It's tempting to round up, skip the bad session or record a lift you cut short as complete. Doing that only fools the one person who needs the data, which is you.

- **Write what you lifted, not what you meant to lift.**
- **Record the rough days.** They're part of the pattern.
- **Note when your form slipped.** A heavier number with sloppy technique isn't a better number.
- **Don't compare your log to someone else's.** Bodies, histories and equipment differ.

## What if you miss a session?

Miss one, write "missed" if you like, and carry on. A gap in a log is normal. Most logs have holes in them, and a few weeks of notes are still worth more than none.

Tracking is a tool, not a test. If it starts to feel stressful, or you feel guilty about a day off, scale it back to the minimum: date, exercise, weight, reps. And if training or numbers are taking over your mood or your week, talk to a doctor or a counsellor you trust.
