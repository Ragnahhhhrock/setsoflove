---
title: "Dating someone who doesn't train: what works"
description: Dating someone who doesn't work out can go well with respect, no pressure to join in, shared interests beyond the gym and honest, relaxed conversations.
---
Dating someone who doesn't work out can go very well, and the recipe is simple: respect their choices, never pressure them to join in, and build your time together around things you both enjoy. Training is a big part of your life, but it doesn't have to be a big part of theirs. The relationship works when each of you gets to be yourself.

## Start by dropping the idea of converting anyone

The most common mistake is treating a partner's lack of training as a project. It comes out in small ways: a pointed comment about their snacks, an invitation to "just try one session" for the fifth time, a gift of workout gear.

None of that is kind, even when it's meant well. People have their own reasons for how they spend their time. They may be busy, or they may prefer walking, dancing or gardening. They may have a health condition, an injury or a history with exercise that you don't know about. They may simply not care for it, which is a perfectly good reason.

A useful rule: if they haven't asked for your help with training, don't offer it. If they do ask, treat it as an invitation and not an opening to take over.

## Talk about it plainly

Say how you train, why you like it and what you'd like from them. Then ask what matters to them. This conversation is easier early, when nothing is riding on it.

Some phrases that help:

- "Training is a big part of my week. I'd like you to know that, so my schedule doesn't surprise you."
- "I don't expect you to join. I'd just like you to be okay with me going."
- "What do you like to do when you have a free afternoon?"
- "Is there anything about my routine that worries you?"

If you want help phrasing the first part, [how to explain your training routine to someone new](/blog/explaining-your-routine-to-a-date/) goes through it step by step.

Listen to the answers. If they say they sometimes feel left out when you're away, believe them and think about what could help, such as a regular shared evening. If they say they're fine with it, believe that too.

## Find shared interests beyond training

Your time together should rest on more than one topic. If your whole relationship is an extension of the gym, it shuts out anyone who isn't part of that world. If it's built on shared enjoyment, it works whatever either person does at 6 am.

Think about activities you can both do without one of you feeling out of place:

- Cooking, eating out or trying new cafes.
- Films, series, books or live music.
- Walking, which suits most people and most budgets.
- Travel, even if it's only a day trip.
- Markets, galleries, board games or a trivia night.
- Time with friends and family.

You can borrow from the ideas in [rest day date ideas that aren't just dinner](/blog/rest-day-date-ideas/), which suit people of any fitness level.

Ask about their interests with real curiosity. You might find you enjoy a hobby you'd never have tried alone, and it's good for you to be a beginner at something. Being the one who's learning, and who is a bit clumsy, tends to build empathy.

## If they want to join you, let them lead

Sometimes a partner gets curious about training on their own. That's lovely, but how you respond counts.

- Let them set the pace and the goal. They might want a walk every day, a class or a couple of light weights sessions.
- Suggest a qualified coach or an introductory session rather than teaching them yourself, unless they ask for your help. It avoids a power imbalance, and a professional can adjust things to them.
- Keep your sessions and theirs separate if that's what works. Training together isn't compulsory. [Training together as a couple](/blog/training-together-as-a-couple/) covers how to do it kindly when both people want to.
- Don't comment on their body or their results. Celebrate that they showed up, and leave it there.

If they try it and don't enjoy it, that's also fine. A "no thanks" is information, not an insult.

## Handle the practical friction

Even with respect on both sides, a few things can cause friction.

1. **Time.** Your sessions take hours out of the week. Agree on the evenings and weekends that are protected for the two of you.
2. **Food.** Eat the way you each like to eat, and keep comments out of it. Nobody needs a running commentary on their plate.
3. **Social plans.** If you're up at 5 am, you may want early nights, and they might not. Work out a pattern that lets you each do both, such as an occasional late night and an occasional early finish.
4. **Money.** Memberships, gear and classes cost money. Agree on a sensible share of the budget for hobbies, whatever they are.
5. **Fatigue and soreness.** Tell them when you're wrecked so they don't read tiredness as disinterest.

When something grates, raise it early and calmly. "I noticed I feel a bit rushed on Saturdays after my session. Could we make Saturday afternoon a proper plan?" gets further than a sigh and a silence.

## Keep your own life as well

Not training doesn't mean your partner has to take the whole load of your life. They have their own routines, and you'll do better if you respect those in return.

Show interest in what they care about. Turn up to the things that matter to them. Make room in your week for them, and be flexible when something unexpected comes up. [Balancing training and a relationship](/blog/balancing-training-and-a-relationship/) goes deeper on calendars, flexibility and shared time.

It also helps to remember that training is one of many ways to look after yourself. Someone who walks the dog, cooks well and sleeps properly is doing something right. Plenty of happy couples have one person in the weights room and one in the garden. What matters is that each feels seen, and that neither is made to feel smaller for what they enjoy.
