---
title: Keeping your routine when you start seeing someone
description: How to keep your gym routine in a new relationship: protect 2 or 3 sessions, stay flexible on the rest and talk about your week early.
---
To keep your gym routine in a new relationship, protect 2 or 3 sessions you won't move and stay flexible about everything else. Tell the person early which sessions those are and why they matter. Then use the rest of your week to spend real time together.

## Why routines slip when someone new arrives

Early dating is exciting, and it takes time. Evenings get booked, sleep gets shorter and the 6:00 am session starts to feel optional. Nothing is wrong with that for a week or two.

The problem comes when it drifts. After a month you realise you've trained twice, you're tired and you feel a bit resentful. That feeling is easy to blame on the other person. It usually comes from never having decided what you'd protect.

You can keep both. It takes a decision made early, not willpower made late.

## Pick your non-negotiables

Choose 2 or 3 sessions a week that are fixed. They're the ones that keep you feeling like yourself. They might be the Monday and Thursday evening lifts, or the Saturday morning run.

A simple way to choose:

1. List the sessions you do in a normal week.
2. Mark the ones that you'd miss most if they vanished.
3. Pick the 2 or 3 that are easiest to repeat at the same time.
4. Write them in your calendar as appointments, with the same weight as work.

Everything else is flexible. If you usually train 5 times a week, the other 2 or 3 sessions can move, shrink or be dropped when you have a good reason.

Keep the number honest. Protecting all 5 sessions in the first month of a relationship isn't realistic, and trying will only make you tense.

## Say it early and say it plainly

You don't need a formal talk. You need a sentence the other person can hold on to.

- "Tuesday and Thursday mornings are my training sessions, so I'm early to bed on Monday and Wednesday."
- "I keep Saturday mornings for a long session. After that I'm free all day."
- "I'm flexible on most things, but I really like not skipping those 3."

Then ask the same of them. What do they protect? It could be a weekly game, a family dinner or a night with friends. Everyone has something. When both people name it, protecting it becomes a shared habit, not a favour.

If your hours are unusual, the posts on [dating when you train at 5 am](/blog/dating-when-you-train-at-5am/) and [dating when you work shifts and still train](/blog/dating-with-shift-work-and-training/) have useful scripts.

## Be flexible on the rest

Being flexible isn't the same as giving in. It means you treat the rest of your week as a pool of time you can move around.

Here is a quick guide.

| Situation | A reasonable response |
|---|---|
| They've had a bad day and ask to see you | Move a flexible session, keep a fixed one |
| A dinner runs late before a fixed session | Go home early, or train later and skip the second thing |
| A friend's event clashes with a non-negotiable | Decide ahead which one wins, and say so |
| You feel run down | Swap a hard session for a walk together |
| You're away for the weekend | Pack a short session you can do anywhere |

A short session is better than none. A 25-minute workout in the morning is often enough to keep the habit alive. If you aren't sure how much to do, ask a coach.

## Combine, but don't force it

Some people love training with their partner. Others like their gym time as their own. Both are normal.

If you'd like to try a shared session, make it optional and keep it light, such as a walk or a class. Don't invite someone to join a session as a test of their commitment. Your own sessions can stay yours.

If you want to know more, [training together as a couple](/blog/training-together-as-a-couple/) looks at when it works and when separate sessions are better.

## Watch for the warning signs

2 things are worth noticing.

First, check whether you're dropping training because you want to, or because you feel guilty saying no. If it's the second, say so. A reasonable person would rather hear "I need that session" than have you resent them.

Second, check whether the other person is asking you to give up training. A request to move one session now and then is normal. A pattern of pushing you to skip, or sulking when you go, deserves a calm conversation. You're entitled to a life that includes the things you value. If it keeps happening, it is a useful piece of information about the relationship.

It also works the other way. If you find yourself using training to avoid dinner with their friends or a hard talk, that's worth a look too. The [balancing training and a relationship](/blog/balancing-training-and-a-relationship/) post goes further into this.

## A realistic week

Here's a sample, to show the idea. It's a template, not a prescription.

- **Monday.** Fixed evening session. Early night.
- **Tuesday.** Flexible. Dinner together.
- **Wednesday.** Rest, or a walk.
- **Thursday.** Fixed morning session. Short, so there is time for the day.
- **Friday.** Free evening for a date.
- **Saturday.** Fixed long session in the morning, then the rest of the day together.
- **Sunday.** Flexible. Chores, a market, a lie-in.

Review it after a month. Ask each other what is working. Adjust one thing at a time.

Keeping your routine isn't selfish. It tends to make you better company, because you arrive rested, clear-headed and glad to be there.
