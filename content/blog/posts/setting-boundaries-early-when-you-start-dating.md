title: Setting boundaries early when you start dating
description: Setting boundaries when dating is easier early on. Learn what boundaries are, how to say them kindly, example phrases and what to do if they're ignored.
---
Setting boundaries when you start dating means telling someone, plainly and kindly, what you are and aren't comfortable with. Doing it early is easier than doing it later, and it tells the other person how to treat you. A boundary isn't a rule for them. It's a decision about what you will do.

## What a boundary actually is

A boundary describes your own limits and your own actions. "I don't give out my address before I've met someone a few times" is a boundary. "You can't ask where I live" is an attempt to control the other person, and it's harder to hold.

Boundaries tend to cover a few areas:

- **Time and pace.** How often you talk, how quickly you meet, how much of your week is already committed to work, family and training.
- **Information.** What you share about your home, workplace, routine and past.
- **Contact.** Which channels you use and when.
- **Money.** Who pays, and the fact that you won't lend or send money to someone you've only just met.
- **Physical contact and consent.** What you're comfortable with, and the right to change your mind at any point.

You don't need to have all of these worked out. Start with the two or three that matter most to you.

## Why saying it early helps

Early conversations are low stakes. Neither of you has much invested, so a clear sentence lands as information and not as rejection. If you wait until something already feels uncomfortable, you're explaining a limit and dealing with the feeling at the same time.

Early clarity also filters well. People who respect a calmly stated preference usually carry on as normal. People who push back, sulk or keep testing it are showing you something useful.

## How to say a boundary kindly

A good boundary statement is short, specific and not an apology. A simple structure is: say what is true, say what you'll do, and offer an alternative if you have one.

Examples you can adapt:

- On pace: "I like talking, and I'm happy to keep chatting a few times a week. I'd rather not talk all day while I get to know someone."
- On meeting: "I'd like to meet in a public place the first couple of times. Coffee near the station works for me."
- On personal details: "I keep my address and workplace private until I know someone better."
- On your routine: "I train at 5:30 am four days a week, so evenings are usually short for me. Weekend mornings are better."
- On touch: "I'm not ready for that yet. I'm enjoying getting to know you."
- On money: "I don't send money to people I haven't met. That's firm for me."

Notice what's missing: long justifications. You can give a reason if you want to, but you don't owe one. "That's not something I'm comfortable with" is a complete answer.

## A short worked example

Say someone you've been chatting with keeps asking which gym you go to and what time you train. You aren't ready to share that yet.

You could write: "I'd rather keep my gym and schedule private for now. I'm happy to tell you more once we've met a few times."

A respectful reply is something like "No worries, that's fair." A reply that should make you pause is "Why don't you trust me?" or a repeat of the question an hour later. The first accepts your limit. The second treats it as a problem to get around. Our post on [keeping your gym and routine private](/blog/keeping-your-gym-and-routine-private/) goes through what to share and when.

## Accept other people's boundaries too

This works both ways. When someone tells you their limit, the response is "Thanks for telling me" and then you act on it. Don't ask them to reconsider, don't test it later and don't take it as a verdict on you. Hearing a limit and respecting it is part of being someone people want to see again.

Consent is also never a one-time thing. Someone can be fine with something one day and not the next, and either of you can change your mind in the middle of a date.

## What to do if a boundary is ignored

Sometimes you say it clearly and the other person carries on anyway. You have options, and you can use them in order.

1. **Say it once more, plainly.** "I've said I'm not comfortable with that. Please stop." Keep your tone steady and short.
2. **Take space.** Stop replying for a while, end the call or leave the date. You don't have to stay to be polite.
3. **End contact.** If it continues, tell them you won't be in touch and stop responding. You don't owe a debate or a final conversation.
4. **Block and report.** Use the block tools on whatever you're using, and report profiles that break the rules. On SetsOfLove, every public profile has a "Report this profile" link.
5. **Tell someone you trust.** A friend can help you think it through and keep an eye out for you.

If you ever feel unsafe, put your safety first and leave. In Australia, [1800RESPECT](https://www.1800respect.org.au/) offers support for people affected by sexual assault, domestic or family violence. If you or someone near you is in immediate danger, call 000.

Ignoring a stated boundary is the other person's choice and the other person's responsibility. It doesn't mean you said it wrong.

## Making it a habit

Boundaries get easier with use. Try stating one small one this week, such as when you're free to talk, and notice how it goes. Pair it with the other safety habits in our [first date safety checklist](/blog/first-date-safety-checklist/), such as meeting somewhere public and having your own way home.

You can always change a boundary as you get to know someone. You decide when, and you decide why.
