description: Deadlift form for beginners: foot position, the hip hinge, bracing and keeping the bar close, plus common mistakes and why a coach is worth it.

Good deadlift form means a bar that starts over the middle of your feet, a braced trunk, a hip hinge and a bar that stays close to your legs the whole way up. Start with a light load, learn the movement slowly and let a qualified coach watch you. The lift looks simple, but the details are easy to miss on your own.

## What is the deadlift, in plain terms?

You lift a loaded bar from the floor until you're standing tall, then lower it back down under control. It's a hinge: your hips move back and forward while your spine stays steady, and your legs and back share the work.

Because the bar starts on the floor, each rep begins from a dead stop. That's where the name comes from, and it's why setup matters so much. There's no bounce or momentum to hide behind.

## How do you set up for the deadlift?

Do the same routine each time, and don't rush it.

1. **Stand with the bar over the middle of your feet.** When you look down, the bar should look like it's cutting your foot in half.
2. **Set your feet about hip width apart.** Toes can point straight ahead or turn out slightly. Whatever feels stable.
3. **Hinge down and take your grip.** Push your hips back, bend your knees as needed, and hold the bar just outside your legs. Your arms hang straight.
4. **Take the slack out of the bar.** Pull gently until you feel the bar tighten against the plates, before the bar leaves the floor.
5. **Brace, then lift.** Take a breath, tighten your trunk and push the floor away.

Your shoulders will usually sit slightly in front of or over the bar at the start. Your hips sit higher than in a squat. If you're not sure, a coach can show you your own best starting position.

## How do you brace properly?

Bracing keeps your spine steady under load. Without it, the bar can pull you out of shape.

- **Breathe in low.** Fill your belly and sides.
- **Tighten around your middle.** Think of it as a firm belt of muscle, not a sucked-in stomach.
- **Keep your ribs down and your neck long.** Look at a spot on the floor a few metres ahead, not up at the mirror.
- **Hold the tension through the lift, then reset at the top or bottom.** Don't let all the air out mid-rep.

Practise bracing without any weight until it feels natural.

## What does good deadlift form look like?

- **Keep the bar close to your body.** It should travel nearly straight up, brushing or very near your shins and thighs. Some people wear long socks to protect their shins.
- **Push through your feet.** Think of pushing the floor away, rather than yanking the bar up.
- **Stand tall at the top.** Hips and knees finish together. You don't need to lean back.
- **Lower the same way you came up.** Push your hips back first, then bend your knees once the bar passes them.

If the bar drifts away from you, it gets harder and the strain moves to your lower back. Close and controlled is the goal.

## What are the common deadlift mistakes?

- **Rounding the back to reach the bar.** If your back rounds as you set up or as you lift, reduce the weight or raise the start height with blocks or plates, and ask a coach.
- **Jerking the bar off the floor.** Take the slack out first, then pull smoothly.
- **Starting with the bar too far from you.** It should be over the middle of your foot.
- **Hips shooting up before the bar moves.** This turns the lift into a stiff-legged pull. Push through the floor with your legs.
- **Leaning back at the top.** Stand tall and stop. Leaning adds strain and no benefit.
- **Dropping the bar from the top.** Lower it with control. Gyms often ask you not to drop it anyway.
- **Adding weight faster than form improves.** The most common one.

## How should you progress?

Start with a light bar, or lift from a raised position (the bar on blocks or on a rack) if the floor is too low to reach with good position. Some people start with a kettlebell or dumbbell version to learn the hinge first.

Add weight in small steps, and only when your last session felt solid. If your form changes, step back. Keep notes so you can see what's working. [How to track your lifts](/blog/how-to-track-your-lifts/) covers what to write down.

Recovery matters with this lift more than most, because it works so many muscles at once. Plan rest days, sleep and the occasional easier week, and don't deadlift to your limit every session.

## Who should you ask for help?

A qualified coach is the best way to learn the deadlift. They can watch from the side, notice small changes in your back position and tell you whether your setup suits your body. Many gyms offer inductions or short coaching sessions, and one or two are enough to get you started.

Stop and see a doctor or physio if you feel sharp pain, pain that doesn't ease, or numbness, tingling or pain that travels down a leg. Don't push through it, and don't wait for it to pass.

If the deadlift sounds like a lot, you don't have to do it. Plenty of people train well without it.
