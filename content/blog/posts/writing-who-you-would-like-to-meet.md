title: How to write the 'who you'd like to meet' section
description: How to write the 'who you'd like to meet' section of a dating profile: positive, specific and kind, built around values rather than a checklist.
---
Write the "who you'd like to meet" section as a short, positive description of the values and habits you enjoy in other people, not a checklist of traits or a list of dislikes. Aim for 2 or 3 sentences that say what you're after and what a good week with someone looks like. Specific and kind beats long and demanding.

## What is this section for?

On SetsOfLove, a profile has two related pieces. One is a pair of selections: who you'd like to meet (any mix of men, women and non-binary people) and what you're after (any mix of dates, a relationship, marriage, friendship or just fun). The other is a few words of your own on who you'd like to meet.

The selections give the facts. The words give the feel. Someone reading your profile should be able to tell, from those few lines, whether your pace and priorities sound like theirs. It isn't an application form. It's a short, honest description.

## Why does positive framing work better?

Compare two versions.

- "No drama, no flakes, no one who skips the gym."
- "I'd like someone steady, who keeps their word and enjoys a routine."

The second says the same thing without a sneer. It tells a reader who you want, not who you've been burned by. Negative lists also tend to rule people out on a technicality: a tired day, a missed session, a cancelled coffee. Positive lines invite the right people in.

A useful rule: for every thing you can't stand, find the thing you'd value in its place. "No arrogance" becomes "someone who's kind to staff and strangers." "No time-wasters" becomes "someone who says what they mean."

## How do you write about values instead of a checklist?

A checklist reads like this: tall, fit, loves travel, wants kids, no smokers. A values-based version reads like this: curious, patient, comfortable with plans that change, happy with a quiet Sunday.

Here is a simple 3-step method.

1. **List what a good relationship, friendship or date looks like for you.** Think of what you'd do together and how you'd treat each other.
2. **Turn each item into a quality.** "Weekend walks" becomes "enjoys being outdoors". "Texting back" becomes "communicates plainly."
3. **Keep the 2 or 3 that matter most.** More than that, and the section turns into a checklist anyway.

One more point on fairness. Avoid describing bodies, ages or looks as requirements. They don't tell anyone who you are, and they can read as unkind to people who'd otherwise suit you well. Keep your focus on how someone is, not how someone looks.

## How do you say what you're after?

Say it once, plainly, and make it agree with your selections. If you picked a relationship and marriage, don't write "just seeing what happens" in the text. If you picked friendship and dates, it's fine to say so and mean it.

Some phrasings:

- "I'm after something long-term with someone who's keen on building a life, not just a fling."
- "Friendship first, and dates if it grows naturally."
- "Open to dates and seeing where it goes, with someone honest about what they want."
- "Looking for easy company and a good laugh, nothing heavy."

The point is clarity. People shouldn't have to guess what you want, and they shouldn't be surprised later. Respect the same clarity from others: if someone's answer differs from yours, that's useful information, not a rejection of you. There's more on this in [dates, a relationship, marriage or friendship: know what you're after](/blog/what-are-you-looking-for/).

## What should you avoid writing here?

- **Contact details.** Phone numbers and social handles aren't allowed in public text fields.
- **Anything obscene or discriminatory.** It will hold up approval, and it says something you probably don't mean.
- **A wall of text.** A few lines are enough.
- **Pressure.** "Message me if you're serious" invites the wrong tone, and there are no messages on SetsOfLove anyway.
- **Assumptions about who reads it.** Write for anyone who might fit, not a single imagined person.

If you want a broader list of things to keep off your profile, [what not to put on a dating profile](/blog/what-not-to-put-on-your-profile/) is worth a read.

## A before and after

Here's a short worked example for someone starting a new chapter.

Before: "Looking for a genuine person who is serious, no games, not into drama, must be fit and love the gym."

After: "I'd like to meet someone warm and steady who's keen on a long-term relationship. Training is part of my week, so a bit of routine suits me, but I care more about kindness, honesty and a good sense of humour."

The second version drops the demand, keeps the intent and speaks to anyone the selections include. It also reads like a person.

## One last read-through

Before you send it for approval, check:

- Does it say what I want, not what I don't?
- Does it fit what I selected for who and what I'm after?
- Would I be happy for a friend to read it out loud?
- Are there any contact details or demands in it?

Once approved, it's yours to share. If you want the wording around the rest of your bio, see [how to write a profile bio when you train](/blog/how-to-write-a-profile-bio-when-you-train/).
