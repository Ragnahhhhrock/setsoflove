You can spot a fake dating profile by looking for photos that seem too polished, details that don't add up, vague answers and pressure to move fast. None of these proves a profile is fake, but several together are a reason to slow down. The most reliable way to confirm someone is real is a live video call, and a mutual friend who knows them.

## What a fake profile usually is

"Fake" covers a few different things. Some profiles use someone else's photos. Some invent a person entirely. Some belong to a real person who has lied about key facts such as age or relationship status. Some are set up to run a scam, where the goal is money or personal information.

Knowing the difference helps. A person who shaved a few years off their age is a problem of honesty. A person who built a persona to draw out your money is a safety problem. The checks below help with both, but the response differs. Stop quickly with the second kind.

## Photos that don't sit right

Photos are the first place to look. Warning signs include:

- Every photo is polished or professionally shot, with nothing casual.
- There are no recent photos, or the photos seem to come from very different years.
- The person seems to look different between photos, beyond hair and lighting.
- Backgrounds have no connection to the life described.
- There's never another person in a photo who's clearly a friend, even though they say they're social.

A reverse image search can show whether the same photos appear under another name. Our guide to [checking if someone's photos are real](/blog/how-to-check-if-someones-photos-are-real/) walks through it. No result doesn't prove the photos are genuine, so treat it as one clue.

## Details that don't add up

Over several conversations, keep an eye on whether the story holds together. Check these gently:

- Their age, suburb, job and routine stay the same from one chat to the next.
- The way they write fits the person described. A profile that says one thing but reads like another deserves a second look.
- Their answers include specifics. Real life has texture, like a particular class, a coach's name or a complaint about the car park.
- Time zones and availability fit where they say they live.

Everyone forgets details occasionally, so don't treat one slip as a verdict. Look for a pattern.

### Vague answers and avoided questions

Ask plain, friendly questions about everyday things: what they did this morning, what they usually train, what their week looks like. A genuine person will answer in their own way. Be wary when:

- Answers stay general or repeat your own words back to you.
- Questions about their life get turned around to you every time.
- They respond with strong feelings very quickly but little detail.
- They say they can't talk by phone or video, and the reasons keep changing.

Some people are shy or private, and that is fine. The concern is the combination of avoiding contact and pushing for closeness.

## Pressure, speed and money

Pressure is the strongest signal. Be careful if someone:

1. Declares strong feelings very early.
2. Wants to move to another app or channel fast.
3. Has an urgent crisis and asks for help.
4. Asks for money, gift cards, bank details or an investment.
5. Gets upset, or goes silent, when you don't go along with it.

Never send money to someone you haven't met in person. If you're being asked, stop and read [romance scam warning signs and what to do](/blog/romance-scam-warning-signs/). In Australia, you can find information and report scams through [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/). If you've already sent money, contact your bank straight away.

## How to verify someone

When something feels uncertain, verify before you invest more time.

- **Suggest a video call.** A short call tells you whether the person looks like the photos. Say, "Want to do a quick video call this week before we meet?"
- **Ask a mutual friend.** If you share a friend or a training circle, ask what they know. See [why introductions through mutual friends can feel safer](/blog/introductions-through-mutual-friends/) for the limits of that too.
- **Search their name and details lightly.** Check whether the things they've told you can be found anywhere. Don't go digging into private matters.
- **Ask a friend to look.** A second opinion helps.
- **Meet in public** when you're ready, with a friend who knows your plans.

If someone is truly genuine, these steps rarely bother them.

## Fake profiles on SetsOfLove

SetsOfLove is a profile app for single gym-goers. Every profile is approved by the admin before it goes live, and the main photo has to show the member's face clearly. Photos with screenshots, memes, logos or other people without consent aren't allowed. There are no messages, so nobody can send you requests for money or ask you to switch to another app through it, and contact details aren't allowed in public text fields.

That reduces some risks. It doesn't verify identity, gym membership isn't checked and lifts are self-reported. If a profile looks wrong, use its "Report this profile" link. ## What to do if you think a profile is fake

You don't have to be certain. If you have doubts, step back, stop sharing personal information and talk to someone you trust. Report the profile on whatever service it's on. If money or personal information has already been shared, contact your bank and report it through [ReportCyber](https://www.cyber.gov.au/).

You're allowed to trust your instincts and walk away from a conversation that doesn't feel right.
