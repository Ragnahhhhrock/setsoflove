Training in summer heat in Australia comes down to a handful of habits: train early or late, drink regularly, protect your skin, ease off when it's harsh and know the warning signs of heat illness. This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, or any heat-related concern, talk to a health professional.

## Does summer change how you should train?

Yes, a bit. Heat makes the same session feel harder. Your heart works harder to cool you down, you sweat more and recovery can drag. None of that means you should stop. It means you adjust.

Think of summer as a season to protect your consistency rather than chase personal bests. A slightly easier session you finish safely is worth more than a heroic one that leaves you wrecked for days. If you lift indoors, you still feel the heat on the way to the gym and in a warm, busy room. Outdoor training adds sun and wind to the mix.

## Pick the right time of day

The simplest change is timing. In most parts of Australia the middle of the day is the harshest, so move your outdoor sessions to the early morning or the evening. Even then, check the forecast, since a warm night or a strong, dry wind can change things quickly.

Some options:

- Train outside at the start of the day, before the sun is high.
- Move to the evening if mornings don't suit your schedule or your sleep.
- Swap a run or ride for an indoor or air-conditioned session on the harshest days.
- Shorten or ease the session when conditions are tough.

If your schedule is already tight, [consistency over motivation](/blog/consistency-over-motivation/) has ideas for keeping a minimum routine without forcing a heavy session on a rough day.

## Drink regularly, before you feel dry

Fluids matter more in the heat. A simple habit is to drink steadily through the day rather than catching up all at once. Carry a bottle, sip between sets and drink some before and after longer sessions.

Look at the colour of your urine as a rough guide. Pale usually means you are doing well, and darker may mean you need more fluid. Don't force huge amounts, though. Drinking far too much in one go is also unhelpful. If you sweat a lot, sweat heavily for long periods or train for hours, ask a doctor or accredited dietitian what suits you.

## Protect your skin

Australian sun is strong, and sunburn is both uncomfortable and a long-term skin risk. For outdoor training:

1. Apply high-protection sunscreen before you head out, and reapply if you are out for a long time or sweating a lot.
2. Wear a hat and sunglasses where your activity allows.
3. Choose light, breathable clothing.
4. Look for shade for rests and warm-ups.
5. Do your outdoor sessions when the sun is lower.

Check your own skin regularly, and see a doctor about any new or changing spots. That's good habit at any age, and it matters more if you spend lots of time outdoors.

## Know the signs of heat illness

Heat illness can build quietly. Watch for signs such as:

- Headache or dizziness
- Nausea
- Heavy sweating, or in a serious case, no longer sweating
- Muscle cramps
- Unusual tiredness or weakness
- Confusion, clumsiness or unusual behaviour
- Fast pulse or fast breathing

If you notice early signs, stop. Move to shade or an air-conditioned space, loosen or remove extra clothing, drink water and cool your skin with water. Don't push on to finish the set.

Confusion, collapse, fainting or a very high body temperature are emergencies. Call 000 straight away. Don't wait to see whether it passes.

For general health information, the national [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/) service is a reasonable place to start, and a GP or sports doctor can advise on your own situation. That matters more if you take regular medication, have a heart, kidney or other health condition, are pregnant, or are returning after illness. Older athletes and those new to training should be extra careful, since heat tolerance varies from person to person.

## Train with a buddy and tell someone

Training with a partner makes heat easier to manage, because you can notice each other's warning signs. If you head out alone, tell someone your route and when you expect to be back. Carry your phone, and avoid isolated spots on very warm days. [How to find a training partner](/blog/how-to-find-a-training-partner/) has ideas if you're looking for company.

Summer is a fine time to scale back intensity, shorten sessions or swap formats. Swimming, an early walk or an indoor class can all keep you moving. Take extra rest, sleep when you can and eat regular meals. If your body is telling you it's had enough, listen.

Heat also affects how you feel about plans outside the gym. If you are meeting someone new, pick a shaded, cooler-hour option, and offer water. [Summer dates by the water in Perth](/blog/summer-dates-by-the-water-in-perth/) has examples of dates that respect the weather.

Before you head out:

- Check the forecast and any warnings
- Choose a cooler part of the day
- Pack water, sunscreen and a hat
- Tell someone your plan if you're alone
- Decide in advance what would make you stop

Common sense is the whole game. Train smart in summer, and you'll still be turning up when the weather cools.
