The core online dating safety tips for Australians are a handful of habits: keep the pace slow, video call before you meet, choose a public place, tell a friend your plans, use your own transport and never send money. None of this needs suspicion of everyone. It's a short routine that keeps you in control while you find out who someone really is.

## Online dating safety tips start with a slow pace

Most of the things that go wrong online rely on speed. Someone who wants intense feelings, private details or a quick decision in the first few days is asking you to skip the part where trust is earned.

A simple decision rule works well: let a person's behaviour set the pace, not their words. Someone who says they're serious but respects "let's talk again tomorrow" is showing you something. Someone who sulks, pushes or turns up the intensity when you slow down is showing you something else.

Set yourself a minimum. For example, no meeting in person until you've had a week or so of regular conversation and at least 1 video call. That's a personal rule, not an official one. Pick numbers that suit you and stick to them.

## Video call before you meet

A video call of even 10 minutes tells you a lot. It confirms the person looks like their photos, that they can talk in real time, and whether you enjoy their company.

If they always have a reason to avoid it, treat that as information. One missed call can be a genuine mix-up. A pattern of excuses, such as a faulty camera, a bad connection or a job that never allows it, is worth taking seriously. Our guide to [checking whether someone's photos are real](/blog/how-to-check-if-someones-photos-are-real/) covers what else to look for.

You can keep the call low-key. Pick a time, say it's a quick hello, and end it when you like. You don't need to show your home or share your address.

## Keep your personal details to yourself, for now

You can have a good conversation without handing over anything that identifies where you live, work or train. In the early days, hold back:

- Your home address and street.
- Your workplace name and roster.
- Your exact training times and which gym you use.
- Financial details of any kind.
- Copies of ID, bank cards or documents.

First names, general interests and your suburb are usually plenty. You can share more as trust builds, and it's your call each time. If someone asks for details you don't want to give, a plain "I'd rather keep that private for now" is a complete answer. How they respond is useful to know.

## Never send money to someone you haven't met

This is the one firm rule. Don't send money, gift cards, cryptocurrency or bank details to someone you've only met online, whatever the reason and however convincing the story. That includes small amounts, loans and "temporary" help.

Requests tend to arrive wrapped in urgency: a medical bill, a travel problem, a frozen account, a business opportunity. A real partner who cares about you won't mind if you say no or ask to talk it through with a friend. A scammer will usually push harder. ## Meet in public, tell a friend, use your own transport

When you do meet, a few simple arrangements make a big difference:

1. Choose a public place with other people around, such as a cafe or a busy park, and suggest it yourself.
2. Tell a friend who you're meeting, where, and when you expect to be home. Send them the person's name and profile link if you have it.
3. Travel there and back on your own. Don't accept a lift from a first date.
4. Keep your phone charged and your drinks in your sight.
5. Plan a way out. A friend who'll call at a set time is easy to arrange and easy to use.

## What to do if something feels off

You don't need proof to leave. If something feels wrong, you can end a conversation, cancel a date or walk out of one. "I've changed my mind, take care" is enough. If you ever feel in danger, call 000.

Some useful places to go next:

- [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/) has information on scams and how to report them.
- [ReportCyber, through cyber.gov.au](https://www.cyber.gov.au/), is where you report cybercrime in Australia.
- The [eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/) can help with online harassment and with images shared without consent.
- Your bank, if you've sent money. Call the number on the back of your card and do it quickly.

Reporting isn't an admission that you made a mistake. It's how patterns get noticed, and it can help the next person.
