title: Seeing an ex at your gym: how to handle it
description: Seeing an ex at your gym is awkward but manageable. Weigh changing times, staying civil, setting boundaries and when to involve staff.
---
If you have an ex at your gym, you have 3 workable options: change your times, stay civil and carry on, or set a clear boundary. Which one fits depends on how the relationship ended and how you feel when you see them. Your routine matters, so aim to protect it without making a drama.

## Is an ex at your gym awkward or unsafe?

This decides everything else, so start here.

**Awkward** means you feel uncomfortable, a bit sad or on edge, but nobody is doing anything wrong. You'd rather not chat. That's normal and it passes.

**Unsafe** means any of these:

- They contact you after you've asked them not to.
- They follow you, wait for you or show up where you train because you're there.
- They comment on you, film you or take photos of you.
- They threaten you or try to intimidate you.
- You feel afraid, whatever the reason.

If it's unsafe, you don't need to handle it alone or politely. Tell gym staff, keep a short record of what happened and when, and leave if you need to. If you're in immediate danger, call 000. For confidential support, [1800RESPECT](https://www.1800respect.org.au/) is there for people affected by sexual assault or family and domestic violence. [Handling unwanted attention at the gym](/blog/handling-unwanted-attention-at-the-gym/) covers more of the practical steps.

The rest of this post is for the awkward version.

## Option 1: change your training times

Moving your session by 30 to 60 minutes is often the simplest fix. Gyms have rhythms, and a different slot may mean a different crowd entirely.

This works well when:

- Your schedules overlap by chance rather than by design.
- You can shift without wrecking your sleep, work or other commitments.
- You'd like a break from the awkwardness for a few weeks.

It works badly when your schedule is fixed by shifts or family. In that case, keep your slot. You were there first, or at least you have as much right to be there as they do.

The same applies the other way. If you're the ex who has kept turning up at the same time out of habit, it's fair to adjust or at least to give the other person room. Never change your time in order to follow someone, and never stay on just to be seen.

## Option 2: stay civil and carry on

For many people, this is the best choice. You don't have to be friends. You just have to be polite, in the way you would to any other regular.

A workable approach:

1. **Nod or say a short hello.** Something like "Morning" is plenty.
2. **Keep it brief.** Skip catch-ups, questions about their life and anything that reopens the relationship.
3. **Stay out of their space.** Choose a different rack or machine if the first choice is next to them.
4. **Don't comment on them to others.** The gym is small. Word travels.
5. **Return to your session.** Your training is the point of being there.

If they're friendly, you can respond in kind and still end the chat after a sentence or 2. If they're cold, don't take it personally and don't chase it.

## Option 3: set a clear boundary

If seeing them keeps unsettling you, or they don't leave you in peace, tell them what you need. Keep it short, calm and specific. For example:

- "I'd like us to keep things to a nod at the gym. Thanks for understanding."
- "Please don't message me. I'd like to train in peace."
- "I need some space. Could you pick a different rack when I'm here?"

You don't need to justify it. If they respect it, that's the end of it. If they don't, you have a pattern worth reporting. Say something like "I've asked for space and it's continuing" to a staff member, and ask what the gym can do.

If you're the one who's been asked for space, treat it as a clear request. Respect it fully and quickly, without arguing, sulking or testing it. That's the whole job.

## When should you talk to staff?

Staff are used to handling this, and most gyms would rather know early. Talk to them if:

- You've asked for space and the person keeps approaching you.
- You feel unsafe or watched.
- The ex is bringing conflict into the gym, such as arguments or comments.
- You want to know what the gym's rules are, and what it can do about shared spaces.

Give the facts: who, what happened, and when. Don't exaggerate and don't understate. Ask what they can do, and whether it's possible to alert the team to the situation. It's reasonable to keep a short note on dates and what was said.

## Protecting your routine

A break-up is hard enough without losing your training, which often helps people feel steadier. Some ways to keep your routine going:

- Have a plan for what you'll do if you see them. A nod, then straight to your first exercise.
- Keep your headphones handy. They're a perfectly good signal that you're busy.
- Train with a friend for the first few weeks if you'd like company.
- Allow yourself a few off days if you need them. Feeling low doesn't mean you've failed.

If you're also thinking about dating again, there's no timetable. [Dating again after a long relationship](/blog/dating-again-after-a-long-relationship/) covers going slowly, and [how to handle rejection gracefully](/blog/how-to-handle-rejection-gracefully/) may help if the break-up was not your choice. If sadness or stress isn't lifting, [Beyond Blue](https://www.beyondblue.org.au/) offers support.

You have as much right to your gym as anyone. A few small, calm habits usually turn "ugh, they're here" into "oh, they're here" within a few weeks.
