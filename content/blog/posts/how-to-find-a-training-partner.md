---
title: How to find a training partner
description: How to find a training partner who fits your schedule and goals: where to look, what to ask, how to stay accountable and how to be a good one.
---
To find a training partner, start with people you already see at the same time each week, ask a clear and low-pressure question, and check that your schedules and goals fit before you commit. A good partner turns up, keeps your sessions on the calendar and respects how you like to train.

## Why bother with a training partner?

Training alone is perfectly fine. A partner just adds a few things that are hard to build on your own.

- **Accountability.** It's harder to skip a session when someone is waiting for you.
- **Company.** Long sessions pass faster with a bit of conversation between sets.
- **A second pair of eyes.** A partner can watch a set and tell you what they noticed, if you've asked them to.
- **Safety.** For some lifts, having someone nearby is more comfortable than going solo.

A partner can also make the gym feel friendlier, which helps if you're still finding your feet.

## Where can you look?

Start close to home.

- **Your regular session.** The people who train when you do already share your schedule. A friendly nod over a few visits is a good start.
- **Group classes.** The same faces turn up to the same class. Partner drills make introductions easy.
- **Friends and colleagues.** Someone you already know and trust might be glad to train together.
- **Local clubs and community sport.** Run clubs, climbing gyms and weekend sport groups are full of people looking for company. If you'd like some ideas, try [parkrun](https://www.parkrun.com.au/), which is free and welcoming to every pace.
- **Gym staff.** Some gyms have notice boards or member groups, and staff may know who else is looking.

Give it time. Friendly regulars become training partners over weeks, not days.

## What should you ask, and how?

Keep the ask small and easy to decline. Something like:

- "I'm usually here around 6:30 am on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Would you want to train together sometimes?"
- "I'm trying to be more consistent. Would you be up for a regular session?"
- "Want to try one session together and see how it goes?"

A one-off trial takes the pressure off both of you. If the answer is no, thank them and carry on as before. A quiet "maybe another time" is also an answer. Don't push, and don't change your own training times to catch someone.

## What should you check before committing?

Fit matters more than friendliness. Talk through these before you settle into a routine:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which days and times can you both do? | A partnership falls apart quickly if schedules drift. |
| What are you each training for? | Different goals are fine, but your sessions need to overlap somewhere. |
| How do you each like to train? | Some people chat between sets, others prefer quiet. |
| What do you do if one of you can't make it? | Agreeing on this early avoids hard feelings. |
| How much feedback do you want? | Some people welcome tips and others would rather ask first. |

A decision rule that works well: if you can agree on 2 regular sessions a week and a way of handling cancellations, you have enough to start.

## How do you keep each other accountable?

Accountability works best when it's gentle and specific.

1. Put the sessions in both calendars.
2. Agree on a simple message to send if you can't make it, ideally a few hours ahead.
3. Check in briefly each month: is this still working for both of us?
4. Celebrate turning up, not just personal bests.

Keep the pressure kind. A partner who guilt-trips you is not helping. If you'd like more ideas for staying on track, [consistency over motivation](/blog/consistency-over-motivation/) has a routine approach that works with or without company.

## How do you be a good training partner?

Be reliable. Arrive when you said you would, or message early if plans change.

Be generous with equipment. Share the bench, rack up weights for each other, and wipe down after use.

Be careful with advice. Offer feedback only when asked. If you see something that looks unsafe, say so calmly and suggest checking with a coach. For anyone new to the big lifts, [the big four lifts explained for beginners](/blog/the-big-four-lifts-explained/) is a good starting reference, though a coach is better.

Respect each other's limits. Some days are harder than others. A good partner doesn't push someone through pain or tiredness.

## What if it stops working?

Partnerships change. People move, switch jobs, change goals or just drift apart. It's fine to end a regular arrangement kindly: "I'm going to try training on my own for a bit, but I've really enjoyed this." You can still say hello at the gym.

If a training friendship turns into something else for either of you, be honest and kind about it. If it doesn't, that's okay too. A good training partner is worth keeping on any terms.
