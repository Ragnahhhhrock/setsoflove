---
title: Progress photos: how to take them and why
description: Progress photos work best as a private, kind record of your training. Learn how to take them consistently, store them safely and use them well.
---
Progress photos are a private record of your training journey. Take them in the same place, in similar light and at a similar time of day, keep them somewhere only you can see, and look back at them with curiosity rather than judgement. They're a diary, not a scoreboard.

## Why take progress photos at all?

Change is slow, and you see yourself every day, so it's hard to notice. A photo gives you a fixed point to look back on. For some people, that's motivating. For others, it's a reminder of how far they've come on a hard week.

They're entirely optional. Plenty of people train for years without taking one. If photos make you feel worse, skip them and track something else, such as how a session felt, how your energy has been or what you've learned. Nothing about your training depends on a camera.

If you do take them, decide up front what they're for. A useful answer is "a record for me". Not a verdict, and not something to show anyone.

## How do you take consistent photos?

The trick is repeating the same conditions, so differences come from you and not from the setup.

1. **Pick one spot.** A plain wall at home or a corner of your bedroom works well.
2. **Use the same light.** Face a window in daylight, or use the same lamp each time. Avoid mixed lighting and strong shadows.
3. **Keep the camera steady.** Lean the phone against something at roughly chest height and use the timer, so you don't have to hold it.
4. **Keep the distance the same.** Mark a spot on the floor with tape if it helps.
5. **Choose the same time of day.** First thing in the morning is easy to repeat.
6. **Wear similar clothing.** Whatever you're comfortable in is fine. You decide what to include.
7. **Use the same poses.** Standing relaxed, front on and side on, is plenty.

Don't pose, flex or suck in. A relaxed photo tells you more, and it's kinder to you.

## How often should you take them?

Less often than you'd think. Daily or weekly photos rarely show change and tend to invite nit-picking. Every 4 to 6 weeks is plenty for most people, and some prefer every few months.

Pick a date and put it in your calendar, like the first Sunday of the month. Take the photos, file them away and carry on with your week. Looking at them once the set is complete tends to be more useful than looking right after you take each one.

Bodies change for many reasons that have nothing to do with training: sleep, hydration, the time of the month, stress, the day's light. A single odd photo means very little.

## What else can you track?

Photos are one tool of many, and often not the most useful. Consider pairing them with notes on:

- How your sessions feel, and whether movements have become smoother.
- Your energy and mood across the week.
- Your sleep. [Rest, sleep and recovery for people who lift](/blog/rest-sleep-and-recovery-for-lifters/) explains why it matters.
- Things you couldn't do before and now can.
- If you log your lifts, the numbers over time. [How to track your lifts](/blog/how-to-track-your-lifts/) covers simple ways to do it.

Often the most telling changes are the ordinary ones. Stairs feel easier. A bag of shopping feels lighter. You enjoy your sessions more.

## How do you look at them kindly?

How you talk to yourself matters more than the photo does. Try these ideas:

- **Look for what's changed in your life, not just your body.** You've been showing up, and that's the part you control.
- **Speak to yourself as you would to a friend.** If you wouldn't say it to someone you care about, don't say it to yourself.
- **Don't compare.** Not with other people's photos, not with edited images online, and not with an imagined ideal. Your only comparison is you at an earlier point in time.
- **Set the photos aside if they upset you.** You can pause, delete or stop at any time.
- **Pay attention to your thoughts.** If photos regularly leave you feeling anxious or low about your body, or if thoughts about food and appearance are taking over, please talk to your GP or another health professional. That's a sign to look after yourself, not to push harder.

Training is for how it feels to move, how strong and capable you feel, and the rest of your life. Appearance isn't the point, and nobody else is grading you.

## Where do you store them safely?

Progress photos are personal, so treat them that way.

- **Keep them in a locked folder or private album.** Most phones have a hidden or locked photo option.
- **Check your cloud backup settings.** Photos can sync to other devices or shared albums without you noticing.
- **Don't send them casually.** Once a photo leaves your phone, you can't control where it goes.
- **Think before you post them.** If you ever want to share, choose carefully and decide who sees them.
- **Delete when you want to.** You can remove old photos whenever you like.
- **Avoid including other people** or identifying details in the background, such as a mailbox or street view.

If someone shares or threatens to share a private image of you without your consent, that's serious. The [eSafety Commissioner](https://www.esafety.gov.au/) has information and a way to report it in Australia.

A photo taken for your own records is different from one for a public profile. If you're choosing images for a profile, gym photos with your face clearly visible are the better fit. Our guide on the [best gym photos for a dating profile](/blog/best-gym-photos-for-a-dating-profile/) covers that separately.

## So, should you start?

If it sounds useful, try one set. Stand in your spot, take a few photos, tuck them in a private folder and set a reminder for 6 weeks. If it feels good, carry on. If it doesn't, stop and don't think of it as failing.

Progress is not only what the camera sees. Your training is yours, and you decide how to keep track of it.
