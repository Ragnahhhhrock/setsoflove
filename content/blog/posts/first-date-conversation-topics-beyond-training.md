---
title: First date conversation topics beyond training
description: Not sure what to talk about? These first date conversation topics lean on curiosity, listening and follow-up questions, so you can skip the interview.
---
Good first date conversation topics are the ones that invite a story: travel, work, food, favourites and small daily rituals. Ask one open question, listen to the answer and follow what they say instead of moving to the next prompt. Training is fine as a topic, but it's a better side dish than a main course.

## Why training can't carry the whole date

You likely have the gym in common, so it's an easy start. The trouble is that it runs out. Sets, programs and personal bests can fill 20 minutes, and then you're both looking at the table.

It can also narrow the picture. You learn about someone's routine but not what they care about, what makes them laugh or what they do on a quiet Sunday.

Give training about 10 minutes, then branch out. Let the gym be the way in, not the whole map.

## Questions that open things up

Open questions invite more than a yes or no. They usually start with "what", "how" or "tell me about".

**Work and daily life**
- "What does a normal week look like for you?"
- "What do you like most about what you do?"
- "What would you do if you could try a different job for a month?"

**Travel and places**
- "Where's the best place you've been, and why?"
- "Is there somewhere you keep meaning to go?"
- "What's your favourite part of living around here?"

**Food and favourites**
- "What's a meal you could eat every week?"
- "What's the best thing you've cooked lately?"
- "Do you have a go-to coffee order?"

**Small stuff that tells you a lot**
- "What's something you've been enjoying lately, like a show, a book or a podcast?"
- "What did you want to be when you were 10?"
- "What's a skill you'd like to learn?"

Pick 3 or 4 you'd honestly like to hear answered. If a question doesn't interest you, skip it.

## Follow-up beats a list

The best conversations don't come from a perfect list. They come from follow-up. Listen for something in the answer that you'd like to know more about, and ask about that.

Say someone mentions a trip to Japan. Don't jump straight to "Do you want to go back?" Ask:

1. "What stuck with you the most?"
2. "What would you do differently next time?"
3. "Was that your first time travelling on your own?"

These show you were listening. Short follow-ups such as "Really? What happened?" or "Why that one?" do the same job.

A rough rule: aim for about half of the talking each. If you realise you've been talking for 5 minutes, hand it back with a question. If you've been answering questions for 5 minutes, ask one of your own.

## Avoid interview mode

Interview mode happens when one person fires questions and the other answers, like a job screening. It feels flat, and it's tiring for both.

Signs you've slipped into it:

- Every turn ends with a new question.
- You're thinking about your next question while they're speaking.
- You don't share anything about yourself.

To fix it, answer your own question. "I love long drives, especially up the coast. What about you?" gives them something to respond to and shows who you are. Share a short story, then ask.

Also avoid topics that put someone on the spot. Early on, leave out detailed questions about past relationships, money and health. If they bring something up, listen and follow their lead. [Dating again after a long relationship](/blog/dating-again-after-a-long-relationship/) has a brief approach for when the past comes up.

## Mentioning training without taking over

Training will come up, and that's good. A few ways to keep it easy:

- Share it as part of your life. "I'm usually up before 6 am, so I'm better at lunch dates" tells them something practical.
- Ask about their routine too, whatever it looks like. They may not train at all, which is fine.
- Skip the numbers unless they ask.
- Keep food and bodies out of the conversation as targets or topics. Order what you enjoy.

## When the conversation stalls

Every date has a flat patch. It's not a sign of failure.

- **Look around.** Comment on the place, the weather or what's on the menu.
- **Return to something they said.** "You mentioned you were learning guitar. How's that going?"
- **Change the setting.** Suggest a short walk. It's easier to talk while you're moving. For ideas, see [Perth first dates: coffee, a walk and nothing fancy](/blog/perth-first-dates-coffee-a-walk-and-nothing-fancy/).
- **Allow the silence.** A pause of a few seconds is normal and often comfortable.

Also, if you're quieter by nature, that's all right. A short date at a calm venue can suit you well.

## Reading how it's going

Pay attention to the other person's energy. Are they asking you questions? Do they lean in, laugh and add details? Or are the answers getting shorter and the eye contact lower?

If they seem to be enjoying themselves, relax. If they seem flat, don't push. It's fine to end a date after an hour with a friendly "I've enjoyed this." If you'd like to see them again, say so simply. If you don't, a kind, honest message later is better than silence.

Finally, remember that the point of a first date isn't to impress. It's to find out whether you'd like to spend more time together. Curiosity, a bit of honesty and a good listener carry you most of the way.
